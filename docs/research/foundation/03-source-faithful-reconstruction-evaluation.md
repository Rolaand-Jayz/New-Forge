# Deep Research: Source-Faithful Multi-Frame Reconstruction

## 1. Executive Assessment  
Multi-frame super-resolution (MFSR) aims to fuse multiple low-resolution (LR) frames into one high-resolution (HR) image by exploiting subpixel shifts. This inverse problem is severely ill-posed, since infinite HR images could map to the same LR inputs under the imaging model.  Early work showed that combining displaced frames can indeed reveal fine detail beyond single-frame interpolation, but only when alignment and blur assumptions are correct. Traditional algorithms range from **shift-and-add** interpolation to advanced optimization (MLE/MAP) and regularization.  More recently, deep networks (e.g. recurrent CNNs, transformers) have achieved impressive visuals. However, the guiding mission here is **measurement-consistent recovery**: any detail in the output must be justified by the source frames.  We must distinguish *genuine recovery* from *hallucinated content* and enforce the forward model.  

This report surveys both classical and learned approaches through September 2026. We analyze *deterministic reconstructions* (shift-add, back-projection, MAP, variational methods, frequency-domain techniques, etc.) with respect to assumptions, noise, alignment errors, and occlusions. We also cover *robust fusion* (e.g. weighted averaging, median filters, M-estimators) and *continuous/inpainting models* (kernel regression, radial basis, implicit fields). We then examine *frequency-aware* strategies (wavelets, multi-band fusion) and *data-consistent learned priors* (PnP/ADMM, DIP/RED, unrolled networks), highlighting how to bound learned contributions by measurements. Learned feed-forward VSR (EDVR, BasicVSR, etc.) is reviewed mainly for fusion capacity rather than as an end solution.  

A critical emphasis is on **evaluation**: devising metrics and tests that reveal whether high-frequency detail is truly supported by the input frames. We outline distortion measures, phase/edge tests (e.g. slanted-edge charts, zone plates), reprojection consistency checks, and protocols for expert visual auditing. Temporal stability and flicker are also key: a per-frame boost that jitters over time is unacceptable for video. Finally, we assess performance trade-offs: which methods can scale toward 30–60 FPS and broad GPU support.  

In sum, the strongest approaches will be those that explicitly incorporate the known forward model and use *provable* reconstruction techniques. Simple averaging or blind deep heuristics may look sharp, but without consistency they risk inventing detail. We will conclude with recommended baseline formulations, evaluation standards, and research pathways to ensure any claimed recovery is truly *source-faithful*.  

## 2. Reconstruction Problem Formulation  
The multi-frame SR problem is formulated as an inverse of an image formation model. Given a set of LR video frames $\{y_k\}$, each is assumed generated from an unknown HR frame $x$ by **blur, downsampling, and noise**: 
$$y_k = D_k * (x * h_k) + n_k,$$ 
where $h_k$ is a blur kernel (e.g. camera point-spread, motion blur), $D_k$ a decimation/downsampling operator, and $n_k$ noise. All frames may have different shifts or motions relative to the latent HR scene. In vector form: $y_k = D_k K_k x + n_k$. Solving for $x$ from multiple $y_k$ is ill-posed – there are many HR candidates consistent with a given set of LR images. Constraints or priors are required to pick a plausible $x$.  

Estimating $x$ thus involves (a) **registration**: find subpixel frame-to-frame shifts/warps; (b) **inversion**: fuse the aligned samples to recover high frequencies beyond the LR sampling limit; and (c) **regularization**: suppress noise and ambiguities. The fused HR should exactly *reproject* back to the LR inputs via the forward model. Mathematically, this often leads to optimization or iterative schemes (MLE/MAP) or direct interpolation. We must track assumptions about motion, blur, noise, and occlusions in each method.  

## 3. Deterministic Inverse Reconstruction  
**Shift-and-Add / Nonuniform Interpolation:** The simplest approach is to align LR frames on a fine grid and average or interpolate (“shift-and-add”).  This essentially merges scattered LR samples into a denser grid, often with a filter (cubic, Lanczos). It is fast and deterministic but limited: if the true scene has frequencies above the LR Nyquist, mere interpolation produces aliasing or blurring.  Shift-and-add assumes perfect knowledge of inter-frame shifts and no blur, so any model error yields artifacts. It can serve as a baseline or initialization, but alone cannot resolve high frequencies that require explicit inversion.  

**Kernel Regression / Weighted Least-Squares:** More sophisticated is *kernel regression*, which fits a smooth HR surface to irregularly-sampled data. Each HR pixel estimates a value by weighted fitting to nearby LR samples (e.g. using an anisotropic kernel). This handles irregular grids and can incorporate confidence weights. It is mathematically related to minimum-norm interpolation or weighted LS.  In essence, one solves for $x$ minimizing $\sum_k \|D_k K_k x - y_k\|^2$ (MLE under Gaussian noise) or variants. With a Gaussian PSF and known shifts, the normal equations have a closed-form solution via conjugate-gradient or direct matrix inversion.  

**Iterative Back-Projection (IBP):** Irani and Peleg pioneered IBP in the early 1990s: starting from an initial HR guess, one iteratively reprojection-corrects it. At each iteration, the HR image is blurred/downsampled to predict LR frames; the residuals are then back-projected (upsampled and smeared) into the HR image to reduce the error. This is akin to iterative tomography. IBP converges to a solution that matches the LR data (if linear) and can incorporate known blur kernels. Compared to naive averaging, IBP is more data-consistent but sensitive to mis-registration: errors in estimated motion or blur propagate through iterations. It is relatively simple and has low per-iteration cost, but may require many iterations for convergence.  

**Projection onto Convex Sets (POCS):** POCS formulates SR as an intersection of constraints. Each LR pixel defines a convex set of possible HR values consistent with it (given the blur/downsampling operator). One alternately projects the current HR estimate onto each constraint set (i.e. enforces each LR equation). In practice, POCS with reasonable initial image often behaves like IBP. It can flexibly incorporate various constraints (non-negativity, known bounds, etc.). Unlike MAP-based LS, POCS can include non-Gaussian/irregular constraints. However, convergence guarantees require convex sets and can be slow if many frames.  

**MLE and MAP Estimation:** Under a probabilistic model, one can estimate $x$ by maximum-likelihood (MLE) or maximum a posteriori (MAP).  MLE assumes a noise model (often Gaussian) and finds $x$ minimizing $\sum_k \|D_k K_k x - y_k\|^2$. This yields a linear normal equation system. MAP adds a prior on $x$ (e.g. smoothness) as regularization: 
$$\min_x \sum_k \|D_kK_k x - y_k\|^2 + \lambda \, R(x).$$  
For example, Gaussian (Tikhonov) prior gives $R(x)=\|\nabla x\|^2$ (L2 smoothing), while robust priors (Huber or Laplacian) yield $R(x)=\|\nabla x\|_1$ or edge-preserving penalties.  MAP is widely used because it explicitly balances fidelity and image plausibility. Convex priors (e.g. quadratic or total variation) give a unique solution; nonconvex (sparseness, MRFs) can be more powerful but harder to optimize.  

**Variational and TV Methods:** Total variation (TV) regularization, which penalizes the L1 norm of the image gradient, is effective at preserving edges. In multi-frame SR, TV or bilateral TV (BTV) has been used to maintain sharp edges while smoothing noise. For example, Farsiu et al. (2004) introduced a *bilateral total variation* regularizer within an L1 data-fidelity framework, achieving crisp edges and robustness to model errors. Variational methods solve the above MAP formulation (often via gradient descent or iterative solvers). They are flexible but computationally heavier than analytic interpolation.  

**Sparse and Frequency-Domain Reconstruction:** If $x$ is assumed sparse in some basis (e.g. wavelets, Fourier), one can enforce that in reconstruction. Frequency-domain methods explicitly try to “de-alias” overlapped spectra: given multiple shifted samples, one can solve for Fourier coefficients that best match all frames. For instance, one can interleave aliased LR DFTs to recover some high-frequency content if shifts are rational. However, noise and non-ideal blur complicate exact Fourier inversion.  Alternatively, one may work in a wavelet or multi-scale pyramid: fuse coarse-to-fine bands across frames. These can focus reconstruction effort on high frequencies while leaving low frequencies to simple fusion.  

**Multi-Frame Deconvolution:** Some SR methods treat each LR frame as a convolution of the HR image by an unknown blur, so they solve a deconvolution-like problem. When blur is large or unknown, blind deconvolution techniques may be nested inside SR. In general, deconvolution-type SR is highly sensitive to PSF errors: a wrong blur estimate leads to ringing or oversharp artifacts.  

**Scattered-Data and MLS Methods:** In irregular sampling situations (e.g. arbitrary bursts), moving least squares or radial basis interpolation can fit a continuous function through the known sample points. These continuous approaches model $x(p)$ as a smooth function (often as a weighted combination of basis functions or polynomials) defined at any coordinate. While conceptually appealing, they require many samples per local region to be stable.  

**Assumptions and Sensitivities:** Every deterministic method makes assumptions: exact frame alignment, known blur, linear image model, no occlusions, etc. If any assumption fails, reconstruction errors emerge. For example, IBP and LS assume the forward model matches reality; model mismatch causes the algorithm to fit noise or generate artifacts. Robust norms (L1 or Huber) alleviate sensitivity to outliers or heavy-tailed noise, but can oversmooth subtle detail. Balancing regularization strength is critical: too much and detail is lost (even if metrics improve), too little and noise/aliasing remains.

## 4. Robust Fusion  
Fusing information across frames requires handling outliers (occlusions, specularities, moving objects) and variable confidence.  The simplest fusion is weighted averaging of aligned pixel values. A robust alternative is the *median* (L1-fusion) of all available samples for each HR location – this resists occasional large errors. More generally, one can use **M-estimators** like Huber or Tukey losses: these act like L2 for small residuals but downweight large discrepancies. For example, L1-norm minimization is equivalent to median and is known to tolerate outliers.  

**Trimmed Means and RANSAC:** Another robust strategy is to discard a fraction of samples with highest residual error (trimmed mean) or to use RANSAC-like schemes: choose subsets of frames that agree, estimate reconstruction, and take consensus. These are expensive in high-dimensional image space but can be applied in local patches.  

**Bilateral/Adaptive Weights:** One can weight each LR sample by a confidence reflecting texture or noise. For instance, weight by local sharpness (blurrier frames contribute less) or by estimated motion reliability. Alternatively, use bilateral-style weights: downweight a sample if its intensity or gradient differs significantly from the reference. In frequency space, one might compute local Fourier consistency: if a particular band is unstable across frames, reduce its weight.

**Statistical Fusion and Confidence:** Since each pixel in HR is supported by a set of LR samples, one can form a local statistical estimate (mean/variance). A low-variance cluster of samples implies high confidence. Fusion can be seen as a small inverse problem itself: solve a weighted least-squares on local samples to estimate the HR value, where weights reflect trust.  Crucially, robust fusion is not just “blend then solve”: it is often better interpreted as imposing a penalty in the global reconstruction (e.g. robust data term in MAP). 

In practice, a hybrid strategy may be best: for static background, average many frames (increasing SNR), but for moving foreground or outliers, revert to spatial-only or drop frames. Fusion may thus vary spatially or adaptively depend on estimated motion/occlusion.  Deterministic baselines like IBP implicitly blend frames via reconstruction; explicit robust fusion methods (like RANSAC or weighted medians) can serve as simpler or oracle baselines.  

## 5. Continuous and Irregular-Sample Reconstruction  
Video frames provide samples of the latent scene at slightly different pixel grids (due to motion). This can be viewed as *scattered data* interpolation in 2D+time. Instead of forcing all samples onto a common grid, one can model the HR scene as a continuous function $f(x,y)$ (or even $f(x,y,t)$) that can be queried at any coordinate. **Kernel regression** is one approach: around each HR point, fit a local polynomial weighted by distance to the neighboring LR samples. **Moving Least Squares** similarly fits local surfaces. These yield smooth reconstructions but require dense sampling in each neighborhood. 

**Radial Basis Functions (RBFs):** Represent $f$ as a sum of Gaussians or other kernels centered at sample points. Then $f$ is analytic anywhere. This handles irregular layouts gracefully, but solving for coefficients can be costly for many points. 

**Differentiable Splatting/Rasterization:** A practical strategy in graphics is to “splat” each LR pixel into the HR plane with a small blur kernel and then blend. Differentiable renderers (neural splatting) allow optimization of both splat weights and scene representation. While powerful, these typically require dense points or incorporate priors to fill gaps. 

**Implicit Neural Representations:** Recent research (e.g. LIIF, INR-based SR) treats the image as a neural net $f_{\theta}(x,y)$ that outputs color for any coordinate. For multi-frame SR, one could train such a net to minimize reprojection error on all frames. This yields a continuous model that inherently handles subpixel shifts. However, naively using MLP-based fields is far too slow for video. Instead, “light” implicit methods (local MLP per patch or coordinate quantization) have been proposed. Their advantage is flexibility and arbitrary upscaling; their downside is high inference cost and potential for overfitting/hallucination without constraints. 

**Discussion:** Continuous reconstructions unify data from all frames without first resampling to a grid. They can naturally handle non-rigid motion or wide disparities. But for real-time SR, most implementations still rely on simpler fusion and grid-based operations. Implicit or continuous models remain largely in research or image SR. In any case, the choice of representation (pixel vs feature grid vs continuous) influences noise handling and aliasing control. Fundamentally, a better representation may reduce artifacts, but does not by itself guarantee data fidelity.  

## 6. Frequency-Aware Reconstruction  
The super-res reconstruction problem is easiest to visualize in the frequency domain: each LR image’s spectrum is the HR spectrum multiplied by the PSF and then aliased (folded) due to sampling. Multiple subpixel shifts between frames correspond to different aliasing patterns, enabling partial separation of frequency components.  A frequency-domain approach attempts to **de-alias** and recover missing high frequencies by solving for a consistent HR spectrum.  

For example, with two frames shifted by 0.5 pixels, certain Fourier coefficients can be linearly solved. More frames with arbitrary shifts lead to a large linear system per frequency. In practice, one can apply a Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) to all LR frames and directly solve for $X(f_x,f_y)$ that best explains the observations under the aliasing equations. This is mathematically equivalent to the spatial-domain least-squares, but spectral methods can isolate issues: if aliasing constraints are underdetermined in a band, reconstruction is uncertain.  

**Multi-band / Wavelet Methods:** Instead of raw Fourier, one can decompose into bands (e.g. Laplacian pyramid or wavelets) and fuse low- vs high-frequency content differently. Low frequencies (below Nyquist) are directly estimable by averaging (since they are sampled in each LR). High frequencies require fusion: one may only partially recover some band, or leave unsolvable bands at low weight. Some methods explicitly compute which spatial frequencies are supported by the temporal evidence. Unsupported frequencies might be filled in by a prior or simply not hallucinated.  

**Phase vs Magnitude:** Some research notes that frame-to-frame consistency of phase is more critical than magnitude for plausible motion. Phase-based registration (estimating subpixel shift via local Fourier phase) is robust for alignment and also suggests that reliable phase reconstruction indicates true structure. If reconstructed high frequencies have inconsistent phase across frames, they are likely hallucinated. 

**Frequency-Selective Weighting:** In robust fusion, one could weight frames differently for different frequency bands: e.g. if one frame is blurred, it contributes to low-pass detail but not high-pass. This suggests designing the fusion operator to account for the PSF: down-weight high frequencies where SNR is low. A complete solution might build an *evidence mask* in the frequency domain, indicating which frequencies are well-supported by the data.  

**Summary:** A frequency-aware pipeline would identify bands where temporal sampling lifts aliasing, reconstruct them from the LR sequence, and refrain from inventing detail in fully aliased bands. Wavelet or pyramid fusion is one practical implementation: align and average coarse layers first, then iteratively inject finer detail only as supported. However, doing this adaptively (per region or per frame) remains an open question.  

## 7. Learned Priors with Data Consistency  
Deep learning has introduced powerful image priors via CNNs and diffusion models. To keep reconstructions source-faithful, we consider hybrid approaches that alternate **data-consistency** with learned denoising. 

**Plug-and-Play (PnP) Methods:** PnP-ADMM alternates between a data-fidelity update (solving a least-squares step with the known forward model) and a denoising step via a pretrained CNN. Thus the algorithm enforces the measurement model while leveraging a learned image prior. Variants include PnP-FISTA, PnP-Red, etc. The advantage is that each intermediate HR estimate remains consistent with the observed frames (up to solver tolerance). The learned denoiser (implicit prior) fills in detail not explicitly provided by data. Critically, one can in principle audit contributions: the fidelity term ensures any introduced feature can be traced back through the denoiser state. In practice, however, it’s hard to quantify exactly how much detail is from data vs prior. Some works try to certify consistency by bounding the difference between predicted and actual LR (see Section 12). 

**Deep Image Prior (DIP):** The DIP framework fits a neural network (usually untrained but randomized) to output an image that matches the LR inputs through the forward model. Early iterations capture broad structures (as in low frequencies), and later iterations may start to overfit noise/detail. If stopped early, DIP can act as a regularizer. DIP is appealing because it is agnostic to training data, but it is slow (network must be optimized per output) and offers no guarantees beyond the chosen stopping rule. Bounding hallucination is difficult; DIP tends to produce smooth results if underfitted, or hallucinations if overfitted. It is more useful as a research curiosity than a deployable baseline for consistent recovery. 

**Regularization by Denoising (RED) / Learned Prox:** Similar to PnP, RED integrates a denoiser into a gradient or proximal operator for the MAP optimization. Learned proximal networks (e.g. unrolled optimization networks) train small CNN blocks to mimic one step of a solver. These networks can be very efficient if well-designed, and they retain an explicit data term in each layer. For example, one can unroll conjugate-gradient or ADMM iterations, replacing the explicit prior with a CNN that takes gradients as input. These unrolled architectures have separate measurement-consistency and learned components, so in theory one could disable the learned part to check pure fidelity. They often achieve state-of-art balance of quality and speed, but complexity grows with unroll depth. 

**Diffusion / Score-based Models:** Denoising diffusion or score-based methods can be conditioned on LR inputs by combining diffusion steps with data consistency. For example, one might run a reverse diffusion process that periodically projects back onto the space of images that reproduce the LR frames when downsampled (a “consistency model”). This yields very photo-realistic outputs, but again bounding the influence of the learned prior is non-trivial. Metrics like reconstruction error (reprojection) can at least indicate consistency.  

For all learned-prior methods, key questions are: *What is enforced by data vs invented by prior?*  Techniques that expose a “data consistency loss” (difference between predicted and observed LR) help audit results. If the forward model is perfect, one can measure a reprojection residual: ideally it is near zero for a truly supported solution. When the forward model is approximate, one may still gauge confidence: large residuals suggest hallucination or model mismatch.  

In summary, learned priors can strongly improve visual quality and fill in plausible detail, but for **source-faithful** goals they must be locked by data consistency. Among these, PnP and unrolled (model-based) networks offer the clearest separation of data and prior, as each iteration explicitly enforces the image formation model. Certified guarantees (e.g. lower bounds on error) are an open research area. Loss functions should include fidelity terms (e.g. LR reconstruction loss) in addition to perceptual or adversarial losses, to discourage unsupported enhancements.

## 8. Learned Feed-Forward Reconstruction  
End-to-end video super-resolution networks have proliferated. Architecturally, most follow: extract features from each LR frame, align features across time, fuse them, and upsample to HR. Key building blocks include **recurrent CNNs** (using a hidden state from previous outputs), **deformable convolutions** (for learned alignment) as in EDVR, and **attention** or transformers to select relevant frames. Frame-recurrent models (e.g. FRVSR) keep a hidden state to propagate detail forward, potentially gathering information over time.  

From the standpoint of *reconstruction versus hallucination*, we must be cautious: these models are trained for visual quality and may “hallucinate” textures not directly supported. Unlike deterministic fusion, there is no explicit data-consistency guarantee. However, modern VSR networks often include a warping loss or optical-flow supervision to encourage temporal coherence.  We note that none of these learned systems inherently enforce the true LR model – they usually assume bicubic downsampling during training. 

A key question: can a learned fusion network extract more legitimate detail than a carefully tuned deterministic method, while still being constrained by data? Possibly, if the network implicitly learns to weight frames or correct alignments beyond simple rules. For example, a learned fusion might smartly average across frames taking into account noise and motion patterns. But if it adds detail, we must ask *from what source?* If the network is small and data-driven, it often defaults to a learned prior. Larger networks (SOTA VSR) often literally “invent” texture. 

Therefore, learned feed-forward methods may be tested as baselines, but any extra detail they produce must be critically evaluated. For source-faithful objectives, a simpler learned module (e.g. a small CNN that denoises or deblurs after a deterministic warp) might be safer than a huge end-to-end SR network.  Extensive ablations should compare such networks against oracle deterministic fusion on ground-truth data to see if genuine high-frequency recovery is happening or just perceptual gain. 

Empirically, many learned VSR methods (BasicVSR++, TecoGAN, etc.) achieve state-of-art PSNR on standard synthetic benchmarks but often introduce temporal inconsistency and ungrounded textures in real scenes. For this project, they might serve as “upper-bound” or candidate modules in hybrid pipelines, but not as drop-in final solutions without strong consistency checks. 

## 9. Adaptive Temporal Support  
How many and which frames to fuse is a crucial design variable. A fixed sliding window (e.g. ±3 frames) is easy but suboptimal if motion varies. In static regions, more frames (even 20+) might help SNR, whereas in fast motion or occlusion zones, only adjacent frames are usable. 

Some research has tried **frame selection**: e.g. drop frames with large motion or blur. Xue et al. (2012) proposed using optical flow to measure inter-frame motion and discard those with too-large displacements before SR. This improves robustness at the cost of available data. Other methods score frames by estimated blur or focus, keeping only the sharpest neighbors. 

Adaptive support can be per-pixel or per-region: for example, use more frames for distant background (stable parallax) and fewer for moving foreground. Confidence maps from alignment (e.g. forward-backward flow consistency) can guide where to trust temporal fusion vs fallback to single-frame. A region that has reached low uncertainty might stop accumulating more frames to save computation. 

There is a risk that incorporating too many frames hurts: if the model (motion, blur) is imperfect, irrelevant or misaligned samples can introduce artifacts. A well-tuned system should detect when adding a frame increases the reprojection error (residual) and thus limit the window. In dynamic scenes, we may even want the system to *reject* all external frames for certain pixels (rely purely on a well-regularized single-frame SR or even just bicubic).  

In summary, **adaptive temporal support** means letting the data decide how much history to use. This could be implemented by confidence-thresholding, gating by movement, or a recurrent state that saturates once no new information is coming. The design should allow long-term evidence (like an LSTM memory) but also reset when the scene changes (shot boundary or new object).  

## 10. Realistic Degradation and Datasets  
Most VSR literature assumes idealized degradation: uniform Gaussian blur followed by bicubic downsampling and additive noise. In reality, camera systems have complex PSFs, sensor noise, lens distortion, Bayer demosaicing artifacts, and video codecs. For source-faithful reconstruction, using unrealistic data is dangerous. 

A number of works have addressed this gap. RealSR and follow-up datasets (e.g. Real-ESRGAN’s paired cameras) capture actual HR-LR image pairs with optical setups. These show that *real* LR images have color shifts, compression artifacts, and spatially-varying blur not present in bicubic models. Training on purely synthetic bicubic LR causes SR networks to fail on real data. 

For video, paired HR-LR is harder: streams with synchronized high/low resolution (like some 4K cameras) are rare. The new StreamSR dataset (360p→1440p from streaming video) provides aligned clips with compression artifacts. This is a more realistic benchmark than REDS or Vimeo-90K for checking performance on real compressed content. Other efforts include simulating “video degradation” by applying realistic blur kernels and noise profiles frame-by-frame, or by using burst-capture from high-speed cameras as pseudo ground truth.  

In any case, an important standard is to evaluate methods on both synthetic benchmarks (where ground truth is known) and on realistic captures (to ensure they generalize). Methods with any learning component should be validated across domains: e.g. train on synthetic and test on RealSR to observe transfer gaps.  

## 11. Reconstruction-vs-Hallucination Evaluation  
We must explicitly test whether recovered details existed in the true HR or were invented. Standard metrics like PSNR/SSIM are inadequate for this purpose; they mix all errors. Instead, specialized tests and visuals are needed:  

- **Known-Frequency Patterns:** Inputs containing standard resolution charts (slanted edges, Siemens stars, zone plates) provide ground-truth frequency content. A source-faithful SR should recover as much of the pattern as supported by the data (e.g. line widths should match physics) and introduce nothing extra. For instance, a slanted-edge test quantifies MTF (true edge spread); zone plates test radial frequency coverage.  
- **Random High-Frequency Textures:** Feed a sequence of random noise or checkerboards into the pipeline. Any “output detail” beyond blurring of the random texture is necessarily hallucinated. Compare the spatial/frequency spectra of output vs ground truth.  
- **Aliasing Tests:** Use a controlled scene with a repeating fine pattern that aliases differently when shifted. If the SR combines frames correctly, the recovered high-frequency alignment will match the true pattern, not create a shifted or warped version.  
- **Text and Glyphs:** High-frequency text lines, sinusoidal gratings at varied orientations/phase, or camera-captured repeating textures can reveal inconsistencies. If an algorithm imposes a learned texture on a blank or blurred region, it will show up as a hallucination.  
- **Inpainting-style Checks:** If one LR frame has a pattern that continues off-frame (like a partial tile), a correct algorithm will not continue it into blank areas without evidence.  
- **Attribution Analysis:** For a given recovered feature (e.g. a visible small fence picket), check which input frames contained any supporting evidence. One could attempt *influence mapping*: disable or perturb one frame at a time and see if that feature vanishes. If no single frame carries the frequency content, it may be a hallucination.  

These tests should be run on synthetic sequences where the exact ground-truth HR is known. They complement perceptual scores: an algorithm might look sharp but fail a frequency test.  

## 12. Data-Consistency Verification (Reprojection Check)  
A powerful validation is to “undo” the SR: take the reconstructed HR image and apply the forward model to generate predicted LR frames. Then compare these predictions to the actual input frames. In an ideal case, they should match (within noise). Large discrepancies indicate the HR image contains unsupported detail or the forward model is wrong. 

Practically, one computes error maps between predicted and actual frames. These residual maps pinpoint where the reconstruction deviates from data (e.g. edges or textures that don’t align). One can accumulate an overall consistency metric (sum of squared residuals) as a proxy for confidence. If the forward model has known uncertainty (e.g. unknown PSF), one can threshold residuals based on expected noise. Regions with low confidence could be flagged or masked. 

Challenges arise if the model is imperfect: for example, if the assumed blur kernel is wrong, even a truthful HR will not match perfectly. In that case, consistency errors will occur even for correct detail. Thus we must study sensitivity: how much model error is tolerable? One approach is to make the forward model part of the estimation, but even so some error remains. 

Nonetheless, reprojection is the most direct fidelity check. It belongs in the evaluation stack: any candidate HR should have a quantified forward-model residual. In a deployment, one could automatically reject or retrain if residuals exceed a threshold.  

Some works embed consistency in the training or inference loop (see Section 7). Another idea is multi-hypothesis: generate several HR candidates (e.g. with different priors) and keep the one with lowest reprojection error. 

## 13. Temporal Stability Evaluation  
A video SR that looks good frame-by-frame can still flicker or wobble in time. Flicker can arise if an algorithm treats each frame too independently, or if alignment errors cause inconsistent fusion. We must assess temporal artifacts explicitly. 

Metrics could include frame-to-frame difference after warping: compute the difference between output frame $t$ and $t+1$ (optical-flow compensated) to measure unnatural changes. One can adapt existing temporal consistency metrics (e.g. using motion estimation or high-frequency energy variance) to super-resolution. For example, [DiffVSR] shows flicker patterns in texture sequences. A simple check is to plot pixel intensities over time at key points: stable regions should have flat traces, not oscillations. 

Human viewing: important scenarios include camera pans (should see static texture move smoothly), or static scenes (no artificial shimmering). Cinematic evaluation (playback at various speeds, in zoom-in on edges/textures) will reveal subtle flickers that metrics might miss. “Ghosting” trails from misalignment can be seen as double-edges for moving objects.  

We should include dedicated temporal tests (e.g. a video of a real scene with static camera, or slowly moving objects) and examine both objective and subjective results. Any method with a heavy reliance on frame-wise hallucination (like per-frame neural enhancement) will likely fail temporal checks, even if per-frame PSNR is high. 

## 14. Human Review  
Automated metrics are necessary but not sufficient. Expert visual review must be part of validation. We recommend blind A/B comparisons against baselines and ground truth, focusing on regions where artifacts often occur: edges, textures, moving objects, and flat areas. 

Standardized viewing conditions: we should specify display resolution, brightness (especially to reveal ringing), and zoom factors. Difference visualizations (e.g. absolute error or edge maps) can highlight subtle discrepancies. Panel reviewers should look for interpolation artifacts, unnatural sharpness, or mismatched textures. 

Human review should have the power to override quantitative scores: if a method has slightly higher PSNR but obvious visible flaws, it should not be chosen. Conversely, a method with modest metric gains but clear better fidelity (less hallucination) can be favored. In any case, documentation of human-perceived issues (with frames or GIFs) should accompany numerical evaluation.  

## 15. Performance Pathway Analysis  
No solution is complete without considering real-time feasibility. For each reconstruction family, we outline an implementation “ladder”: from ideal high-quality (ignore speed), to optimized but complex, to streamlined for 30–60 FPS. 

- **Iterative Methods (IBP/MAP/POCS):** Typically involve many iterations of warp+back-projection or CG solves. The oracle implementations may use all frames and full-resolution convolutions – extremely slow. A quality-first prototype might use tens of frames and full-precision FFTs. To reach real-time, one would need to prune: use fewer frames (e.g. 5–7), limit iterations (e.g. 1–3 back-proj steps per frame), exploit separable filters, lower precision, and aggressive parallelism. GPU-suitable? Yes, convolution and sampling can be GPU-accelerated. Still, a fully iterative approach pushing all detail recovery may remain too heavy for 60 FPS on 1080p.  
- **Robust Blending (weighted average/median):** Very fast in principle: just a few parallel accumulations. Oracle version might consider all frames, but real-time can limit to nearest frames. GPU-friendly. A baseline robust fusion (median per pixel or bilateral filtering) could run at 4–8 ms per HD frame if implemented well.  
- **Variational / TV:** Solving a large system (with FFT or multigrid) is computationally intensive. A few iterations of primal-dual algorithms might suffice for moderate quality. Multi-resolution approaches (solve coarse, then refine) can speed up. Possibly 2D convolutions and simple arithmetic per pixel - amenable to GPU. Still, expensive regularizers (nonlinear) add overhead. Unlikely to hit 60 FPS without major concessions (e.g. one shot of TV on 4× downsampled grid).  
- **PnP/Unrolled:** In the oracle form, one might unroll 5–10 iterations of ADMM with a heavy CNN denoiser each time. At high resolution, this is huge. However, recent work (USRNets) shows a few iterations with a compact CNN can run fairly fast (~30 ms per frame on good GPUs). Simplifying each step (e.g. using half-precision, tiling, smaller models) could bring it nearer to 30 FPS.  SiMVP or DSP methods might accelerate this family. Key issue: memory footprint and multiple warps per iteration. It's plausible that with aggressive optimization a 30 FPS prototype (with small CNNs and 4-frame windows) could be built; 60 FPS would be very challenging unless using low-res stages or hardware acceleration.  
- **Feed-Forward CNNs/Transformers:** Many state-of-art VSR nets already run near realtime at 1080p using current GPUs (e.g. BasicVSR++, EDVR achieve ~20–30 FPS on RTX-class hardware). A stripped-down version (fewer residual blocks, lighter alignment) can hit 60 FPS. These are naturally GPU-amenable (batch linear ops). However, their ability to yield only measurement-consistent output is dubious – they excel in perceptual quality rather than fidelity. From a performance standpoint, they are promising, but caution: their internal behavior is not constrained by physics, so any speed gains come at the risk of more hallucination unless metrics / filters constrain them.  
- **Continuous/Implicit Methods:** Raw INRs or RBFs are far too slow for live video. However, a clever parameterization (e.g. neural field with voxel grid, or multi-resolution hash encoding) could be sped up. Likely not realistic for 30 FPS at anything beyond small resolutions.  

In summary, the most promising for real-time are either very optimized CNN-based fusion (learned feed-forward) or a single-shot analytical fusion (robust blending or single-iteration back-projection). Iterative and unrolled methods will probably remain heavier and used offline or at lower resolution.  

## 16. Experimental Ladder  
We propose staged experiments of increasing realism (with known ground truth at each stage):  

1. **Stage 1 – Ideal**: Known HR $x$, known shifts, known blur/downsampling, no occlusion. Generate LR by $y_k = D(K x)$. Test reconstruction with and without noise. This isolates algorithmic capacity: does shift-add or IBP recover full frequency content as theory predicts? Use toy data (e.g. textures, edges). Exit criterion: algorithm reaches theoretical bound of detail for given frame count.  
2. **Stage 2 – Estimated Alignment**: Same model as Stage 1 but remove access to true shifts. Use an optical-flow or feature-based registration step. Evaluate sensitivity to misregistration: small shift errors can cause large artifacts. Success requires robust performance even when alignment is estimated.  
3. **Stage 3 – Unknown Degradation**: Remove knowledge of blur/PSF. Now $K$ is unknown or approximate. The algorithm must estimate or adapt to blur. Evaluate how mismatch degrades result. Test methods that jointly estimate blur or use blind deconvolution.  
4. **Stage 4 – Dynamic Scenes**: Introduce independent motion/occlusion: e.g. moving objects or parallax. Use synthetic ground-truth for which pixels are static background vs moving foreground. Test if the algorithm can separate static vs dynamic content. Ideally, static parts exploit all frames, while moving parts rely on spatial priors or single frame SR. Ground-truth segmentation can be used to measure correct adaptation.  
5. **Stage 5 – Compression and Noise**: Encode LR frames with realistic video codecs (H.264/H.265) and add sensor noise. See how quantization blocks or compression artifacts lead to inconsistent details. Methods with robust fusion or explicit noise modeling should hold up better than naive approaches.  
6. **Stage 6 – Real Video**: Test on real captured sequences (with some proxy ground truth, e.g. high-resolution frame at lower framerate). Use the most realistic datasets (e.g. StreamSR, RealSR video pairs, or own captures). Compare against strong SISR baseline. Here the goal is not perfect fidelity (since true HR may be ambiguous) but consistent improvement in perceptual quality and fidelity checks.  

At each stage, compare: (a) a strong single-frame SR method (e.g. deep image SR or Lanczos) as baseline; (b) a deterministic multi-frame algorithm (e.g. IBP or robust fusion); (c) learned candidates (PnP or VSR net) if justified. Document failure modes. The ladder helps isolate which assumptions break which methods, and define clear "exit criteria" for each stage.

## 18. Architecture Implications  
Our findings suggest a **hybrid baseline**: Start from a data-driven reconstruction algorithm (e.g. one or a few steps of IBP or LS fusion) and optionally refine with a small learned module that does not override data consistency. For instance, a pipeline might warp frames, compute a weighted LS solution (one pass), then apply a light CNN denoiser constrained by the forward model (as in data-consistent PnP).  

Specifically:  
- **Scientific Baseline:** A good reference is a *weighted least-squares solution with L1/L2 blending*. This uses the known model to fuse frames optimally, with robust norms to handle noise and minor misalignments. It is simple to implement (FFT or multi-grid) and sets a fidelity standard.  
- **Deterministic Priorities:** Iterative back-projection deserves implementation first as an oracle: it directly enforces consistency and can serve as a benchmark. Robust fusion (median or M-estimator) is very attractive for static regions. We also recommend a frequency-aware fusion (e.g. solve for supported bands via FFT) as an oracle test.  
- **Learned Components:** Small learned modules can add value if they respect measurements. For example, a CNN that refines the LS output, or a learned demosaicing/denoising step inside an iterative solver, could be useful. General video SR nets (EDVR, etc.) are not recommended for final use, but insights from them (like attention to alignment confidence) could inform weights.  
- **Signal Domain:** Evidence leans toward doing reconstruction in **pixel/grid space** with possibly a multi-scale decomposition. Operating entirely in feature space risks hiding details. Some continuous modeling could be useful conceptually (e.g. implicit warping for alignment), but practical systems will likely remain on pixel or wavelet grids.  
- **Adaptive Support:** Yes – allow the algorithm to choose how many frames to fuse per region. A mechanism (e.g. threshold on consistency gain) should turn off temporal fusion when not beneficial.  

## 19. Remaining Unknowns  
Despite this survey, some questions remain open:  

- **Optimal Fusion vs Selection:** Is it better to drop frames dynamically or to always use them with proper weighting? The trade-off between selection and fusion strategies needs empirical study.  
- **Forward Model Errors:** Even the best SR algorithm will fail if the blur model is wrong. Quantifying how much mismatch can be tolerated, or how to learn PSF on the fly, is critical.  
- **Quantifying “Supported Detail”:** Beyond binary tests, can we derive a metric that measures the *amount* of detail truly recovered (e.g. compare output and input spectra directly)? This would help tune priors.  
- **Learned Prior Boundaries:** How to formally constrain a learned model so it only adds detail where data allow? Ideas include test-time dropout or confidence gating, but no consensus yet.  
- **User Tolerance:** It remains subjective how much hallucination (if any) is acceptable in practical use-cases. Are perceptual gains worth a small risk of non-supported detail? This may vary by application (e.g. security vs entertainment).  

Overall, the central question (“What evidence in the source supports this detail?”) must guide future work. Every algorithm and evaluation scheme we develop should explicitly address that question, not just by intuition but by measurable criteria.
